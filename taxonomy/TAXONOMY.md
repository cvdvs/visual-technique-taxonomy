## How it reads

- **Operation**: What the image asks the viewer to do.
- **Domain**: What the image works through to get there.
- **Technique**: The specific mechanism that does the work.
- **Execution**: References in your own library that use the technique: the evidence.

Read top to bottom, it is the order to think in when working a brief: what should the viewer do, what will the image work through, which mechanism, and which of your references prove it works.

## The four operations

**Differentiate**: What is different here? Make one thing stand out from everything around it. The viewer notices a difference before anything else.
Briefs that need it say things like: "stand out", "the hero", "unmissable", "pop", "different from the competition", "the one finding that matters", "focus on".

**Group**: What belongs together? Make separate things read as one set, family or system. The viewer sees units instead of pieces.
Briefs that need it say things like: "a family", "these go together", "a system", "unified", "order many parts", "one brand across", "relationships".

**Sequence**: Where do I look next? Control the order of looking. The viewer is led first here, then there, then there.
Briefs that need it say things like: "guide the eye", "first see", "hierarchy", "step by step", "a story", "flow", "explain in order".

**Infer**: What does this mean beyond what I see? Make the viewer complete the meaning: a hidden shape, a metaphor, a reference, a missing moment. The idea is supplied by the viewer, not shown.
Briefs that need it say things like: "without saying it", "clever", "an idea", "a metaphor", "a wink", "surprise", "they should get it".

## The eight domains

- **Feature**: Visible properties of marks: light and dark, hue, saturation, temperature, size, shape, texture, sharpness. Works before you start thinking.
- **Space**: Where things sit and how much room they get: position, emptiness, depth, overlap, framing, cropping.
- **Gestalt**: The rules the eye uses to organize parts into wholes: nearness, enclosure, connection, continuity, symmetry, completion. Named after the Gestalt psychologists who described them.
- **Social**: Our automatic reading of people: faces, eyes, gestures, bodies. We follow gazes and see faces whether we mean to or not.
- **Semantic**: What the things in the image are and what they mean, and what happens when meanings are paired, merged or swapped.
- **Cultural**: Shared references the viewer brings: genres, famous images, learned symbols, reading direction, the look of how things are made.
- **Time**: Change across frames, or movement implied in a still: animation, motion graphics, comics and manga panels, images that hold several moments.
- **Encoding**: Data mapped onto visual variables such as position, length, color and count, in charts, maps and diagrams.

### Differentiate

#### Differentiate through Feature

**Chiaroscuro** `chiaroscuro`

Strong light against deep shadow. The lit part becomes the subject and the dark swallows everything else.

- Why it works: The eye lands first on the strongest local contrast of light and dark, and darkness removes competing detail.
- What changes: value
- How to spot it: Large areas near black, one directional light, the subject modeled by light rather than by outline.
- Use it when: Drama, gravity, intimacy, or one subject pulled out of a busy world.
- It fails when: Light is spread evenly or the shadows go muddy gray, so nothing is singled out.
- Not to confuse with: A silhouette is a flat dark shape with no interior; rim light is a thin bright edge from behind; glow is light that seems to come from the thing itself. Chiaroscuro models form with light falling on it. Tie-breaker with chiaroscuro and glow: when the light comes from a direction and models the form, it is chiaroscuro; when the figure is the brightest thing in the picture with no visible source, it is glow.
- Foundation: Baroque tenebrism (Caravaggio); Itten's contrast of light and dark, The Art of Color (1961)
- Related: Silhouette, Rim light, Isolation in void

**Rim light** `rim-light`

A thin line of light around the subject's edge, usually from behind, that separates it from a dark background.

- Why it works: The bright outline defines the shape even when the front of the subject is in shadow.
- What changes: value
- How to spot it: A glowing edge on hair, shoulders or the contour of an object, with the light coming from behind.
- Use it when: A dark subject must still read on a dark ground; cinematic, nocturnal or premium product moods.
- It fails when: The rim is the only light and the subject reads as a cutout, or the background is light and the rim disappears.
- Not to confuse with: A thin bright edge from behind. A broad soft halo around the subject is glow.
- Foundation: Studio photography and film lighting: the back light of three-point lighting
- Related: Chiaroscuro, Silhouette

**Silhouette** `silhouette`

The subject reduced to a solid shape against a contrasting ground, recognized by its outline alone.

- Why it works: We recognize things from their contour, so removing interior detail makes the outline carry the whole message.
- What changes: shape, value
- How to spot it: A flat dark (or light) shape with no interior detail and a clear, readable edge.
- Use it when: Iconic, instantly recognizable forms; mystery; reading from far away.
- It fails when: Limbs or objects merge into one blob, so the outline stops being readable.
- Not to confuse with: Chiaroscuro keeps the form modeled by light; a silhouette is flat.
- Foundation: Profile silhouette portraits (18th century, named after Étienne de Silhouette); Rubin on figure and ground (1915)
- Related: Chiaroscuro, Rim light, Figure-ground reversal

**Pop-out** `pop-out`

Many similar items and one that differs in a single property, so it is found instantly.

- Why it works: A difference in one simple feature (color, tilt, size) is spotted before deliberate looking, across the whole field at once.
- What changes: hue, orientation, size, shape
- How to spot it: A field of near-identical things with one odd one out.
- Use it when: Find-the-one ideas: the different product, the outlier, the rebel in the crowd.
- It fails when: The odd one is defined only by a combination of features that each also appear in the crowd (a red circle among red squares and green circles); then it has to be searched for instead of popping out.
- Not to confuse with: If the odd one differs in a feature (color, size, shape), it's pop-out; if it breaks the arrangement (lifted, rotated, out of line, a different kind of thing), it's a pattern break. When both, label the stronger. A saturation accent needs no crowd, just one vivid color in a muted image.
- Foundation: Treisman & Gelade, feature integration theory (1980)
- Related: Pattern break, Saturation accent, Repetition

**Saturation accent** `saturation-accent`

One vivid, saturated color inside an otherwise muted or gray image: one element (even a single element in several hues), or a few small accents of the same hue.

- Why it works: With everything else quiet, the only intense color takes all the attention.
- What changes: saturation
- How to spot it: A desaturated or monochrome scene with one element in full color, like a red coat in a gray street.
- Use it when: Singling out a product, a person or a detail while keeping the setting visible.
- It fails when: Several elements are saturated, or the accent color repeats elsewhere and dilutes it.
- Not to confuse with: Highlight and mute is the same move inside a chart or text. Tie-breaker for the color techniques. Saturation accent: one vivid element (one hue, a few small accents of the same hue, or a single multicolored element) in an otherwise muted or gray image. Pop-out needs a crowd of similar items with one odd one; saturation accent needs only a muted image. Warm–cool split: temperature separates subject from setting, whichever side is the warm one (a cool figure on a hot red field counts). Complementary pairing: two opposite hues together carry the image, usually one dominant and one in a smaller share. Value accent is the same move made with lightness instead of color.
- Foundation: Itten's contrast of saturation, The Art of Color (1961)
- Related: Pop-out, Highlight and mute, Complementary pairing

**Warm–cool split** `warm-cool-split`

Warm colors set against cool ones to separate subject from setting, or one zone from another.

- Why it works: Warm hues seem to come forward and cool ones to fall back, so temperature alone can create figure and depth.
- What changes: temperature
- How to spot it: Orange skin against a teal background, a warm lamp in a blue room, a frame split into a warm and a cool half.
- Use it when: Separating subject from setting without light-dark contrast; night scenes; two opposing camps.
- It fails when: It is applied as a default color grade with no reason, or warm and cool mix in both zones.
- Not to confuse with: Tie-breaker for the color techniques. Saturation accent: one vivid hue (one element, or a few small accents of the same hue) in an otherwise muted or gray image. Warm–cool split: temperature separates subject from setting, whichever side is the warm one (a cool figure on a hot red field counts). Complementary pairing: two opposite hues together carry the image, usually one dominant and one in a smaller share.
- Foundation: Itten's contrast of warm and cool, The Art of Color (1961)
- Related: Complementary pairing, Simultaneous contrast

**Complementary pairing** `complementary-pairing`

Two opposite hues (red and green, blue and orange, yellow and violet) built into the image so each makes the other more intense, usually one dominant and one in a smaller share.

- Why it works: Opposite hues give the strongest hue contrast, and each looks more saturated next to the other.
- What changes: hue
- How to spot it: The image is carried by two dominant opposite colors.
- Use it when: Maximum vibrancy; a bold poster; a product set against its complementary color.
- It fails when: Both colors take equal space and fight, so edges vibrate and nothing dominates. Give one a small share.
- Not to confuse with: Tie-breaker for the color techniques. Saturation accent: one vivid hue (one element, or a few small accents of the same hue) in an otherwise muted or gray image. Warm–cool split: temperature separates subject from setting, whichever side is the warm one (a cool figure on a hot red field counts). Complementary pairing: two opposite hues together carry the image, usually one dominant and one in a smaller share.
- Foundation: Chevreul, De la loi du contraste simultané des couleurs (1839); Itten's complementary contrast (1961)
- Related: Warm–cool split, Saturation accent, Simultaneous contrast

**Simultaneous contrast** `simultaneous-contrast`

Surrounding colors change how a color looks: the same gray reads warm on blue and cool on orange, light on black and dark on white. The image is built on that interaction.

- Why it works: We judge color and lightness relative to their surroundings, never in isolation.
- What changes: hue, value
- How to spot it: Colors that only make sense together; one color that seems to glow or shift because of what surrounds it.
- Use it when: Making a small palette feel rich; making one color glow by choosing its surroundings; optical play.
- It fails when: It happens by accident: a brand color shifts on a new background and looks wrong.
- Foundation: Chevreul (1839); Albers, Interaction of Color (1963)
- Related: Complementary pairing, Warm–cool split

**Scale disparity** `scale-disparity`

An extreme difference in size between elements, so the giant or the tiny one runs the image.

- Why it works: Size difference is read instantly, and it tells a story: power and smallness, ambition and vulnerability.
- What changes: size
- How to spot it: A tiny figure next to a huge object, or one element vastly bigger than everything else.
- Use it when: Showing power, loneliness, ambition, or how much one thing matters.
- It fails when: The sizes differ only a little, which reads as a mistake rather than an idea.
- Not to confuse with: Size hierarchy grades sizes to set a reading order; scale disparity uses one extreme gap to make a point.
- Foundation: Arnheim, Art and Visual Perception (1954), on visual weight
- Related: Size hierarchy, Isolation in void, Exaggeration, Viewpoint

**Texture against flat** `texture-against-flat`

A richly textured element set against a smooth, flat field, or a flat shape against a busy textured one, so the change of texture separates subject from ground.

- Why it works: A change of texture splits the image into figure and ground before anything else is read.
- What changes: texture
- How to spot it: Fur, crumpled paper, rough stone or a grainy photo on a clean flat color; a photo cutout on a flat graphic.
- Use it when: Making material and craft felt; product shots; collage mixing photography and flat graphics.
- It fails when: The background has its own texture or noise and competes.
- Not to confuse with: Label it only when the texture change is what separates the subject from its ground. Dither, halftone and noise count as texture when they read as a surface. If the texture is there to show how the image was made, that's visible facture.
- Foundation: Julesz, textons and texture segregation (1981)
- Related: Visible facture, Isolation in void

**Selective focus** `selective-focus`

Only the subject is sharp; everything around it is blurred.

- Why it works: Sharpness signals importance, so the eye settles on the sharp area and skips the blur.
- What changes: sharpness
- How to spot it: Shallow depth of field: a soft background or foreground and one sharp plane.
- Use it when: Close attention to one detail; separating a subject from a cluttered real setting; intimacy.
- It fails when: Focus lands on the wrong thing, or the blur wipes out context the story needs.
- Foundation: Photographic depth of field
- Related: Isolation in void, Depth layering

**Glow** `glow`

An element seems to give off light: a bright core fading softly into darker surroundings.

- Why it works: A soft gradient around a bright center makes the eye read it as a light source, so it outshines everything that only reflects light.
- What changes: luminance gradient
- How to spot it: Neon, backlit halos, screens and orbs glowing on dark, light leaking around a subject.
- Use it when: Energy, technology, the sacred, night; making one element the source of attention.
- It fails when: Everything glows, or the glow muddies edges and text.
- Not to confuse with: Rim light is a thin edge from behind; chiaroscuro models form with light falling on it; glow is light that seems to come from the thing itself. Tie-breaker with chiaroscuro and glow: when the light comes from a direction and models the form, it is chiaroscuro; when the figure is the brightest thing in the picture with no visible source, it is glow.
- Foundation: Zavagno, the glare effect (1999)
- Related: Rim light, Chiaroscuro, Saturation accent

**Exaggeration** `exaggeration`

The defining feature pushed beyond reality: huge eyes, a stretched limb, an enormous product, a caricatured face.

- Why it works: We recognize things by what sets them apart; pushing that difference further makes recognition faster and memory stronger.
- What changes: size, shape, proportion
- How to spot it: Proportions no real body or object has, distorted in the direction of the subject's most typical trait.
- Use it when: Character design, caricature, mascots, product hero shots, satire.
- It fails when: The wrong trait is pushed, so the subject becomes less recognizable instead of more.
- Also serves: Differentiate through Social; Infer through Semantic
- Foundation: Ramachandran & Hirstein, the peak shift principle, The Science of Art (1999)
- Related: Scale disparity, Anthropomorphism

**Value accent** `value-accent` (new in v1.4)

One light shape in an otherwise dark image, or one dark shape in an otherwise light one, so the odd value takes the eye without any color doing it.

- Why it works: Lightness contrast is read before hue; when everything sits in one key, the single shape outside it is found first.
- What changes: value
- How to spot it: A white face inside a black hood, a pale figure in a night scene, a few black accents in a white room, one dark chair on a pale floor.
- Use it when: Singling out a subject in monochrome or near-monochrome work; quiet focus without color.
- It fails when: Several shapes share the odd value, or the rest of the image isn't kept in one key.
- Not to confuse with: Saturation accent does this with one vivid color; value accent does it with lightness alone, in any palette. Chiaroscuro models form with light and shadow across the whole image; value accent sets one shape apart by its value. Value massing organizes the whole image into a few big light and dark shapes; value accent is one small shape against one large key. Pop-out needs a crowd of similar items with one odd one. Glow is light coming off the thing, a bright core fading into its surroundings; value accent is a pale shape that stays a shape, with no light spreading from it. When light spreads, label glow.
- Foundation: Itten's light-dark contrast, The Art of Color (1961); Livingstone, Vision and Art: The Biology of Seeing (2002)
- Related: Saturation accent, Chiaroscuro, Value massing, Pop-out

Also used this way: Highlight and mute (main entry under Differentiate), Visible facture (main entry under Infer).

#### Differentiate through Space

**Isolation in void** `isolation-in-void`

The subject alone in a large empty space.

- Why it works: With nothing to compete with, the single object gets all the attention, and the emptiness adds weight and calm.
- What changes: position, empty space
- How to spot it: A small or medium subject in a big plain field, usually with more than half the frame left empty.
- Use it when: Luxury, focus, solitude, one idea or one product; quiet editorial pages.
- It fails when: The empty space gets filled with logos and copy later, or the subject is too small to read.
- Not to confuse with: Center placement is about position on the axis; isolation is about the emptiness around the subject, centered or not.
- Foundation: Ma, the meaningful interval in Japanese aesthetics; von Restorff's isolation effect (1933)
- Related: Center placement, Selective focus, Saturation accent

**Center placement** `center-placement`

The subject placed dead center, often frontal, so it becomes the still heart of the image.

- Why it works: People tend to look at the center of an image first, and a centered subject reads as important, stable and direct.
- What changes: position
- How to spot it: Subject on the central axis, even margins, little competing around it.
- Use it when: Icons, portraits with authority, product heroes, confrontation, ceremony.
- It fails when: The image needs movement or story; centering then feels static or stiff.
- Also serves: Sequence through Space
- Foundation: Arnheim, The Power of the Center (1982); Tatler, the central fixation bias (2007)
- Related: Symmetry, Isolation in void, Direct gaze

**Frame within a frame** `frame-within-frame`

A doorway, window, mirror, screen or cut-out frames the subject a second time inside the picture.

- Why it works: The inner frame isolates the subject and adds depth: the eye passes through the outer layer into the inner one.
- What changes: position, depth
- How to spot it: Subject seen through a door, window, arch, mirror, screen or shaped opening.
- Use it when: Intimacy or voyeurism, focus, a story inside a story, layered depth.
- It fails when: The inner frame is weak or cut off, or the frame is more interesting than what it holds.
- Also serves: Sequence through Space
- Foundation: Painting and film composition, e.g. the doorway view in Vermeer's The Love Letter (c. 1669)
- Related: Depth layering, Isolation in void, Occlusion

**Split comparison** `split-comparison`

One frame divided to show with and without, before and after, or two options side by side, the same in everything except what is being compared.

- Why it works: When everything else is held equal, the single difference is impossible to miss.
- What changes: position
- How to spot it: Half-and-half images, with and without a filter or product, A versus B layouts.
- Use it when: Proving an effect, product demonstrations, research comparisons.
- It fails when: Other things change between the halves too, so the comparison is muddy.
- Not to confuse with: Temporal synthesis compresses several moments of one process; a split comparison sets two states side by side to compare them.
- Foundation: Tufte on keeping comparisons within one glance, Envisioning Information (1990)
- Related: Semantic contrast, Small multiples

**Viewpoint** `viewpoint` (new in v1.3)

The eye placed unusually low, high or tilted, or part of the subject thrust at the lens, so the angle itself changes how big, powerful or close the subject feels.

- Why it works: We read size and power from where we stand: looking up makes a figure loom, looking down makes it small, and a hand foreshortened at the lens seems to reach out of the picture.
- What changes: camera height, tilt, foreshortening
- How to spot it: Figures seen from below against the sky, scenes from above, a tilted horizon, a foot, fist or blade huge in the foreground.
- Use it when: Making a hero, product or threat dominant; making a figure feel small or watched; adding unease or energy.
- It fails when: The angle is mild or arbitrary, so it reads as a framing accident rather than a choice.
- Not to confuse with: Scale disparity compares sizes inside the picture; viewpoint changes how big the subject feels through where the eye stands. Forced perspective uses distance to fake a relation between two things.
- Also serves: Infer through Social
- Foundation: Mandell & Shaw, camera angle and how people are judged (1973); Meyers-Levy & Peracchio, Getting an angle in advertising (1992)
- Related: Scale disparity, Forced perspective, Direct gaze

**Frame break** `frame-break` (new in v1.3)

Part of the subject crosses the edge of its own frame, panel, card or border, so it seems to step out of the picture toward the viewer.

- Why it works: A frame tells the eye where the picture ends; whatever crosses it reads as nearer than the picture and more alive than what stays inside.
- What changes: overlap, position
- How to spot it: An arm or blade over a panel border, an illustration spilling past its card, a painted scene running onto its mount.
- Use it when: Making a character, product or idea burst out; depth in flat layouts; drama in comics and covers.
- It fails when: Too many things break the frame, so the frame stops meaning anything.
- Not to confuse with: Crop at the edge cuts the subject off so the viewer completes it outside; frame break lets it cross the frame and come closer. Frame within a frame keeps the subject inside a second frame.
- Also serves: Infer through Space
- Foundation: Trompe-l'œil escapes from the frame (Pere Borrell del Caso, Escaping Criticism, 1874); breaking the panel in comics (McCloud, Understanding Comics, 1993)
- Related: Crop at the edge, Frame within a frame, Occlusion

**Halo framing** `halo-framing` (new in v1.3)

Rings, rays, a nimbus or a radiating pattern behind a central figure that frame it and seem to shine from it at once.

- Why it works: Radial lines and rings all point to their center, so the figure in the middle is found first and seems to give off the radiance around it.
- What changes: arrangement, luminance gradient
- How to spot it: Haloes and mandorlas, sunbursts behind a figure or product, concentric rings, rays fanning from a head.
- Use it when: Sacred or iconic presence, heroic portraits, product reveals, making one figure the center of a world.
- It fails when: The pattern is busier than the figure, or the figure sits off its center.
- Not to confuse with: Glow is light coming from the thing itself; halo framing is a drawn structure around it. Frame within a frame encloses the subject; halo framing radiates from it. A halo as a sign of holiness is also a learned symbol; label halo framing when its shape organizes the picture. Tie-breaker with leading lines: lines that converge on a subject elsewhere are leading lines. Glow and halo framing can both stand on one radiant element: glow for the light itself, halo framing for the drawn rings or rays that organize the picture around the center.
- Also serves: Sequence through Space
- Foundation: The nimbus and mandorla in religious art; Arnheim, The Power of the Center (1982)
- Related: Glow, Center placement, Frame within a frame, Learned symbol

Also used this way: Graphic overlay (main entry under Infer).

#### Differentiate through Gestalt

**Pattern break** `pattern-break`

A regular pattern (a grid, a row, a rhythm) with one element that breaks the rule.

- Why it works: Repetition sets up an expectation, and the single violation gets noticed and remembered.
- What changes: arrangement, orientation, substitution
- How to spot it: Rows of identical items with one turned, missing, out of line, or replaced by a different kind of thing.
- Use it when: Be-the-exception messages; standing out in a category; showing the anomaly.
- It fails when: The pattern isn't regular enough to set an expectation, so the break doesn't register.
- Not to confuse with: Pop-out changes one feature of one item; a pattern break breaks the arrangement itself or swaps in a different kind of thing.
- Foundation: von Restorff, the isolation effect (1933)
- Related: Pop-out, Repetition

#### Differentiate through Social

**Direct gaze** `direct-gaze`

A face or eye (human, animal or drawn) looking straight out, meeting the viewer's eyes.

- Why it works: Eye contact is detected fast and holds attention, and it makes the viewer feel addressed.
- What changes: gaze direction
- How to spot it: Eyes aimed at the lens, often the only face looking out while others look away.
- Use it when: Confrontation, intimacy, testimony, a call to act; covers and portraits.
- It fails when: Every face looks out so none stands out, or the eyes are too small or shadowed to read.
- Not to confuse with: A gaze cue looks at something inside the picture and sends your eye there; a direct gaze looks at you.
- Foundation: von Grünau & Anston, the stare-in-the-crowd effect (1995)
- Related: Gaze cue, Center placement

Also used this way: Exaggeration (main entry under Differentiate).

#### Differentiate through Semantic

**Semantic contrast** `semantic-contrast`

Two things with opposite meanings put together so each sharpens the other: life and death, luxury and trash, nature and machine.

- Why it works: Placed side by side, opposites force a comparison, and the clash is the message.
- What changes: subject meaning
- How to spot it: A pairing that feels charged or wrong: a gas mask on a bride, a flower in a rifle barrel, healthy forest beside a clear-cut.
- Use it when: Provocation, consequence, before and after, irony, cause campaigns.
- It fails when: The opposition is too subtle to read, or so obvious it says nothing new.
- Not to confuse with: Side-by-side analogy pairs things for their likeness; semantic contrast pairs them for their opposition.
- Foundation: Phillips & McQuarrie, juxtaposition for opposition, typology of visual rhetoric (2004); Barthes, Rhetoric of the Image (1964)
- Related: Side-by-side analogy, Fusion

#### Differentiate through Encoding

**Highlight and mute** `highlight-and-mute`

In a chart, diagram or block of text, the one series, point or phrase that matters gets the color or full ink; everything else goes gray.

- Why it works: Muting the context makes the story line the first thing the eye finds, while the rest stays available for comparison.
- What changes: saturation, value
- How to spot it: Gray lines or bars with one in a strong color; a headline set mostly in gray with the key words in full ink.
- Use it when: One finding must stand out among many; explanatory charts; research visuals that make one claim.
- It fails when: Several things are highlighted, or the muted context is so faint it can't be read.
- Not to confuse with: Use the Feature path for text and interfaces, the Encoding path for charts and diagrams. One word in color with the rest in plain white or black counts too, as long as the rest is uniformly quiet. Saturation accent is the same move in a picture. Two-tone headlines count only when the emphasized part is the key phrase. Weight alone counts too: bold key words in a light sentence.
- Also serves: Differentiate through Feature
- Foundation: Knaflic, Storytelling with Data (2015); Treisman & Gelade (1980)
- Related: Saturation accent, Annotation, Direct labeling

**Common baseline** `common-baseline`

Values compared as positions or lengths on one shared scale, so differences can be read precisely.

- Why it works: People judge position along a common scale more accurately than angle, area or color.
- What changes: position, length
- How to spot it: Aligned bars, dot plots, values on one axis instead of pies, bubbles or color shades.
- Use it when: Small differences matter; the reader must compare exactly; honest evidence.
- It fails when: Baselines differ between parts (stacked middles, broken axes), so comparisons quietly mislead.
- Foundation: Cleveland & McGill, Graphical Perception (1984)
- Related: Small multiples, Highlight and mute, Size encoding

**Color scale** `color-scale`

A data value mapped to color along a ramp (light to dark, or two colors meeting at a midpoint), as in heatmaps and choropleth maps.

- Why it works: Ordered lightness reads as more and less, so patterns across a whole field show at a glance.
- What changes: value, hue
- How to spot it: Heatmaps, choropleths, gradients keyed to a legend, fractals colored by iteration count.
- Use it when: Patterns across many cells, geography, density.
- It fails when: A rainbow ramp invents boundaries the data doesn't have, or lightness doesn't follow the order.
- Not to confuse with: Count it only when lightness or a two-color ramp reads as more and less. A rainbow of iterations is decoration unless it carries order. Categorical hue gives each kind its own color with no order.
- Foundation: Bertin, Sémiologie graphique (1967); Harrower & Brewer, ColorBrewer (2003)
- Related: Categorical hue, Common baseline

**Size encoding** `size-encoding` (new in v1.3)

A value shown by the size of a mark: bubble area, the width of a ribbon or line, the size of a symbol on a map.

- Why it works: Bigger reads as more at a glance, so size ranks values instantly, though exact areas are judged poorly.
- What changes: size
- How to spot it: Bubble charts, Sankey flows, proportional symbols on maps, tree maps.
- Use it when: Showing rough magnitude and ranking at a glance across many items.
- It fails when: Exact comparisons matter (area is judged less accurately than position or length), or marks overlap until they can't be read.
- Not to confuse with: Common baseline compares values by position or length on one scale, more precisely; color scale maps values to color; size encoding maps them to area or width.
- Foundation: Bertin, Semiology of Graphics (1967); Cleveland & McGill, graphical perception (1984)
- Related: Common baseline, Color scale, Scale disparity

Also used this way: Small multiples (main entry under Group).

### Group

#### Group through Feature

**Color blocking** `color-blocking`

Large flat fields of solid color organize the image; everything sharing a color reads as one zone, even when far apart.

- Why it works: Shared features bind things together: similar color, shape or texture is grouped before it is read.
- What changes: hue
- How to spot it: Bold flat color areas, outfits or objects matched to their backgrounds, color used to sort parts into sets.
- Use it when: A product family, a system, a set that should read as one; fashion and editorial layouts.
- It fails when: Too many colors, so the blocks no longer sort anything.
- Not to confuse with: Color blocking groups through flat fields of color; for any other shared treatment (palette, texture, shape language), use similarity.
- Foundation: Wertheimer, the law of similarity (1923)
- Related: Common region, Repetition

**Similarity** `similarity`

Different elements share one treatment (palette, shape language, texture, frame or type), so they read as one family.

- Why it works: Shared features bind things into groups before they are read one by one.
- What changes: hue, shape, texture, treatment
- How to spot it: Brand boards, product families, a series with one consistent finish, diagram parts coded by a shared color.
- Use it when: Systems, campaigns, sets that must read as one.
- It fails when: The shared trait is too weak against the differences between items.
- Not to confuse with: Color blocking groups through flat color fields; repetition repeats the same unit; similarity binds different units through a shared treatment.
- Foundation: Wertheimer, the law of similarity (1923)
- Related: Color blocking, Repetition, Categorical hue

**Value massing** `value-massing`

The image organized into a few large shapes of dark and light, so it reads at a glance as one strong pattern.

- Why it works: Merging many parts into a few value masses simplifies the picture into a structure the eye grasps instantly.
- What changes: value
- How to spot it: Big flat blacks against white (spotted blacks in ink and manga), a figure and its shadow merged into one dark shape.
- Use it when: Posters, ink illustration, manga, logos; impact from far away.
- It fails when: Mid-tones break the masses into many small pieces.
- Not to confuse with: Chiaroscuro models one subject with light; value massing designs the whole picture as a few flat lights and darks.
- Foundation: Notan, brought into Western design teaching by Arthur Wesley Dow, Composition (1899)
- Related: Silhouette, Chiaroscuro, Color blocking

**Tone on tone** `tone-on-tone` (new in v1.5)

The whole image kept inside one color family, tints and shades of a single hue or its close neighbors, so nothing stands out by color and the picture reads as one calm whole.

- Why it works: With hue held still, the eye stops sorting by color; small shifts in lightness, material and light carry the interest, and the shared color reads as a single mood.
- What changes: value, saturation, texture
- How to spot it: Cream-on-cream and beige-and-oak rooms, an outfit in shades of one color, a poster in tints of one hue, a room washed in one lamp's color, near-monochrome film or anime frames.
- Use it when: Calm, softness, luxury and restraint; letting form, texture and light carry the picture; a quiet field that makes a later accent land.
- It fails when: Something breaks the family hard enough to become an accent (then label the accent), the palette goes so flat that the forms disappear, or the single hue is only a process (an old sepia print, a duotone) rather than a choice that carries the image.
- Not to confuse with: Saturation accent and value accent set one thing apart by color or lightness; tone on tone sets nothing apart. Arbitrary color is a hue the subject could not have; tone on tone is about how few hues the image allows, natural or not. Similarity binds separate items into a family through a shared treatment; tone on tone keeps one picture inside one color family. Value massing organizes light and dark into big shapes; tone on tone is about hue. Black-and-white images are not tone on tone: there is no hue to hold back.
- Also serves: Infer through Feature
- Foundation: Monochromatic and analogous harmonies (Itten, The Art of Color, 1961); American Tonalism (Corn, The Color of Mood: American Tonalism, 1880–1910, 1972); tone-on-tone styling in interiors and fashion
- Related: Saturation accent, Value accent, Arbitrary color, Similarity

Also used this way: Cast-light pattern (main entry under Infer).

#### Group through Gestalt

**Proximity** `proximity`

Elements placed close together read as one group, separate from elements further away.

- Why it works: The eye binds near things into units before reading them one by one.
- What changes: distance
- How to spot it: Clear clusters, with bigger gaps between clusters than inside them.
- Use it when: Organizing many items into a few understandable sets: layouts, product families, data clusters.
- It fails when: Gaps inside groups are as large as the gaps between them.
- Foundation: Wertheimer, laws of perceptual organization (1923)
- Related: Common region, Connection

**Repetition** `repetition`

The same element repeated many times, so the set reads as one pattern with its own rhythm.

- Why it works: Repeated identical units fuse into a texture or a row, and the repetition itself becomes the image.
- What changes: count, arrangement
- How to spot it: Grids or rows of the same object, face, product or mark.
- Use it when: Abundance, mass production, a system, insistence; setting up a pattern to break.
- It fails when: The units vary too much to fuse, or the repetition has no point and reads as wallpaper.
- Not to confuse with: Repetition is the same unit side by side; self-similarity is the same form nested inside itself at smaller scales; similarity binds different units through a shared treatment.
- Also serves: Sequence through Gestalt
- Foundation: Wertheimer, the law of similarity (1923)
- Related: Pattern break, Small multiples, Pop-out

**Common region** `common-region`

Elements inside the same container (a box, card, panel, color field or outline) read as a group, even when far apart.

- Why it works: A shared boundary overrides distance and similarity in deciding what belongs together.
- What changes: enclosure
- How to spot it: Cards, panels, boxes, tinted areas or outlines holding sets of items.
- Use it when: Interface layouts, product sets, separating sections without drawing lines.
- It fails when: The containers are so many and so alike that each item just sits alone in its own box, or boxes nest so deep that nothing groups.
- Not to confuse with: Bento grids count when each cell groups the things inside it (a stat with its label, an image with its caption). Proximity groups by distance alone; common region groups by a shared container.
- Foundation: Palmer, Common Region (1992)
- Related: Proximity, Color blocking

**Connection** `connection`

Lines, threads or paths physically link elements, so linked things read as one structure.

- Why it works: Connected elements are seen as a unit, strongly enough to override distance and color.
- What changes: links
- How to spot it: Node-and-line diagrams, strings, cables, roads, flowcharts, constellations.
- Use it when: Relationships, networks, systems, cause and effect.
- It fails when: So many crossing links that the structure turns into a tangle.
- Not to confuse with: Use the Encoding path when the links carry data (a network or flow diagram), the Gestalt path when they're compositional.
- Also serves: Group through Encoding
- Foundation: Palmer & Rock, uniform connectedness (1994)
- Related: Good continuation, Proximity

**Good continuation** `good-continuation`

Elements arranged along a smooth line or curve read as one continuous path, and the eye follows it.

- Why it works: We prefer smooth, unbroken contours, so aligned parts are seen as one line.
- What changes: alignment, direction
- How to spot it: Objects lined up on an invisible curve; a path, river or row that bends through the frame.
- Use it when: Tying separate parts into one flow; leading toward a product; graceful compositions.
- It fails when: Gaps or sharp angles break the line so it reads as separate pieces.
- Not to confuse with: Leading lines are actual lines in the scene pointing at a subject; good continuation is the eye joining separate elements into one line.
- Also serves: Sequence through Gestalt
- Foundation: Wertheimer (1923)
- Related: Leading lines, Connection

**Symmetry** `symmetry`

Elements mirrored around an axis, so the two halves read as one balanced whole.

- Why it works: Symmetric arrangements are seen as single, complete figures, and they feel stable, formal and deliberate.
- What changes: arrangement
- How to spot it: Mirror-image layouts, a central axis, frontal architecture and faces.
- Use it when: Authority, ceremony, calm, perfection; logos and emblems.
- It fails when: Near-symmetry looks like a mistake, or symmetry is used where tension is needed.
- Foundation: Koffka, Principles of Gestalt Psychology (1935)
- Related: Center placement

**Self-similarity** `self-similarity`

The same form nested inside itself at smaller and smaller scales: fractals, branching, spirals of spirals.

- Why it works: One rule generating every scale makes the whole read as a single system, and suggests it goes on forever.
- What changes: scale
- How to spot it: Fractal renders, trees and rivers branching, recursive layouts.
- Use it when: Growth, nature, infinity, complexity from a simple rule.
- It fails when: The levels don't visibly share the form, so it reads as noise.
- Not to confuse with: Repetition is the same unit side by side; self-similarity is the same form inside itself.
- Also serves: Infer through Gestalt
- Foundation: Mandelbrot, The Fractal Geometry of Nature (1982)
- Related: Repetition, Connection

**Density emergence** `density-emergence` (new in v1.3)

A form built up from many marks, lines or points that pile up into a shape no single mark draws: string-art envelopes, point clouds, attractor plots, forms that exist only as dot or character density.

- Why it works: The eye reads where marks crowd as solid and where they thin as edge, so thousands of lines become one curved surface.
- What changes: density, count
- How to spot it: Straight lines whose crossings make a curve, particle shells, plots of millions of points, forms made of text or dots.
- Use it when: Generative and data art; a whole emerging from many parts; delicate volume without outlines.
- It fails when: The marks are too sparse to fuse, or so dense the form flattens into a blot.
- Not to confuse with: Closure asks the viewer to complete a gap; density emergence gives a complete form made by accumulation. Repetition repeats a unit you still see as a unit; here the units disappear into the form. Halftone and dot grids are visible facture when the dot texture is the trace of a process; density emergence when the dots build a form no outline draws. Stipple used only as shading inside an outlined drawing is not density emergence.
- Foundation: Gestalt grouping (Wertheimer, 1923); curve stitching, popularized by Mary Everest Boole (c. 1900)
- Related: Closure, Repetition, Visible facture

#### Group through Social

**Face schema** `face-schema`

Unrelated elements arranged so they read as a face: two dots and a line, a car front, a power outlet.

- Why it works: We are wired to find faces in the simplest configurations, and once seen, the face holds the parts together.
- What changes: arrangement
- How to spot it: Objects, layouts or marks whose placement suggests eyes and a mouth.
- Use it when: Making objects friendly or alive; mascots, packaging, playful logos.
- It fails when: The face is too faint to notice, or appears by accident where it undermines the message.
- Not to confuse with: Decide by how it reads. If the parts read first as something else (a car front, an outlet, a layout) and a face emerges from them, it's face schema. If it reads first as a character with a face, it's anthropomorphism.
- Also serves: Infer through Social
- Foundation: Face pareidolia; Johnson & Morton, Biology and Cognitive Development: The Case of Face Recognition (1991)
- Related: Anthropomorphism, Closure

#### Group through Semantic

Also used this way: Fusion (main entry under Infer).

#### Group through Time

**Common fate** `common-fate`

Elements that move together, in the same direction and at the same speed, read as one group.

- Why it works: Shared motion binds things more strongly than looking alike.
- What changes: motion
- How to spot it: In motion: particles, flocks or interface parts moving as one. In stills: blur or streaks pointing the same way.
- Use it when: Showing that separate things act as one system; transitions that regroup elements.
- It fails when: Too many groups move at once, or the motion is too subtle to notice.
- Foundation: Wertheimer, common fate (1923)
- Related: Implied motion, Proximity

Also used this way: Match cut (main entry under Infer).

#### Group through Encoding

**Small multiples** `small-multiples`

The same small chart or image repeated in a grid, each panel showing one slice: a year, a place, a category.

- Why it works: Identical frames fuse into one set, so the eye ignores the frame and compares only what changes.
- What changes: data slice
- How to spot it: A grid of same-size, same-scale mini charts, maps or photos.
- Use it when: Comparing across many categories, change over time, variants in a series.
- It fails when: Scales differ between panels, or panels are too small to read.
- Not to confuse with: Small multiples compare slices of one measure in a shared frame. A grid of pictures that isn't comparing anything (a character lookbook, a type weight ladder) is similarity or repetition instead.
- Also serves: Differentiate through Encoding
- Foundation: Tufte, Envisioning Information (1990)
- Related: Repetition, Common baseline

**Direct labeling** `direct-labeling`

Labels sit right on or beside the marks they name, instead of in a separate legend.

- Why it works: Nearness binds the label to its mark, so the reader never has to look back and forth.
- What changes: position
- How to spot it: Series names at line ends, values on bars, names on map regions, no legend box.
- Use it when: Any chart that must be read quickly or by non-specialists.
- It fails when: Labels collide or cover the data.
- Not to confuse with: Direct labeling names a mark; annotation adds a takeaway or an interpretation.
- Foundation: Tufte, The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983); Wertheimer on proximity (1923)
- Related: Proximity, Annotation, Highlight and mute

**Categorical hue** `categorical-hue`

Each category or series gets its own hue, so everything of one kind can be followed across a chart or diagram.

- Why it works: Distinct hues separate categories without implying an order, and same-hue marks group across distance.
- What changes: hue
- How to spot it: Multi-series line charts, colored node types in a network, legend-keyed categories.
- Use it when: Comparing a handful of categories; following series through a dense chart.
- It fails when: More than six or seven hues, or hues that differ only slightly.
- Not to confuse with: A color scale implies more and less; categorical hue only says 'different kind'.
- Foundation: Bertin, Sémiologie graphique (1967)
- Related: Color scale, Similarity, Direct labeling

Also used this way: Connection (main entry under Group).

### Sequence

#### Sequence through Feature

**Size hierarchy** `size-hierarchy`

Elements graded by size and weight, so they are read biggest first, then down the ranks.

- Why it works: Larger and heavier elements are seen first, so a clear ladder of sizes becomes a reading order.
- What changes: size, weight
- How to spot it: Headline, subhead, body; a large lead image with smaller supporting ones.
- Use it when: Any layout with more than one message: posters, editorial pages, interfaces.
- It fails when: Too many similar sizes, so no clear order emerges.
- Foundation: Müller-Brockmann, Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981)
- Related: Scale disparity, Reading path

#### Sequence through Space

**Leading lines** `leading-lines`

Lines in the scene (roads, rails, edges, arms, shadows), or drawn lines radiating toward it, point to the subject.

- Why it works: The eye travels along lines and edges to where they converge.
- What changes: direction
- How to spot it: Converging perspective lines, diagonals that end at the subject, paths into the frame.
- Use it when: Pulling the eye to something small or far away; depth; a sense of journey.
- It fails when: Lines lead out of the frame or to empty space, taking attention with them.
- Not to confuse with: Manga focus lines, drawn radiating toward the subject, count as leading lines. A pointer is a sign (an arrow, a cursor); leading lines are part of the scene or its drawing. Tie-breaker with halo framing: rays or rings that radiate from a central figure and frame it are halo framing; lines that converge on a subject somewhere else are leading lines. Never label the same rays as both.
- Foundation: Zettl, graphic vectors, Sight Sound Motion (1973)
- Related: Good continuation, Body vector, Gaze cue

**Depth layering** `depth-layering`

Clear foreground, middle ground and background planes that the eye moves through, near to far.

- Why it works: Overlap and size cues stack the image into planes, and we read them in order of distance.
- What changes: depth, overlap
- How to spot it: A foreground element framing or partly covering the scene (a branch, a shoulder, a railing), then the subject, then the distance.
- Use it when: Immersion, scale, a sense of being inside the scene.
- It fails when: The foreground is so strong it steals the subject's attention.
- Not to confuse with: Distance shown by planes fading into haze or fog (aerial perspective) counts too. Occlusion is one thing hiding another; depth layering is the whole scene stepping back in planes.
- Foundation: Repoussoir in landscape painting; Zettl (1973)
- Related: Frame within a frame, Occlusion, Selective focus

Also used this way: Center placement (main entry under Differentiate), Frame within a frame (main entry under Differentiate), Reading path (main entry under Sequence), Occlusion (main entry under Infer), Halo framing (main entry under Differentiate).

#### Sequence through Gestalt

Also used this way: Repetition (main entry under Group), Good continuation (main entry under Group).

#### Sequence through Social

**Gaze cue** `gaze-cue`

A figure looks at something in the image, and the viewer's eye follows the look.

- Why it works: Seeing someone look somewhere shifts our own attention there almost automatically.
- What changes: gaze direction
- How to spot it: Faces or eyes turned toward a product, a headline or another person.
- Use it when: Directing attention to text or a product; story between characters.
- It fails when: The gaze points away from what matters, or out of the frame.
- Not to confuse with: A direct gaze looks at the viewer; a gaze cue looks at something in the picture. A figure seen from behind, looking into the scene so that we look with them, is a viewer stand-in.
- Foundation: Friesen & Kingstone, gaze cueing (1998)
- Related: Body vector, Direct gaze, Leading lines, Viewer stand-in

**Body vector** `body-vector`

Pointing arms, leaning bodies and directions of movement lead the eye along their line.

- Why it works: We read bodies as having direction, and follow where a gesture or posture aims.
- What changes: direction, pose
- How to spot it: Outstretched arms, pointing fingers, running or leaning figures, a dancer's line.
- Use it when: Energy, action, steering attention through a scene.
- It fails when: Gestures point in many directions and cancel each other out.
- Not to confuse with: Body vector is where a pose sends the eye; implied motion is a still reading as moving. One striding figure can do both; label both only if each does real work.
- Foundation: Zettl, index vectors (1973); Arnheim (1954)
- Related: Gaze cue, Leading lines, Implied motion

**Viewer stand-in** `viewer-stand-in` (new in v1.3)

A figure seen from behind, looking into the scene, so the viewer looks with them and takes their place (the Rückenfigur).

- Why it works: A turned back hides the face we would read and offers its point of view instead; we step into the figure and see what it sees.
- What changes: pose, position
- How to spot it: A small figure with its back to us facing a vast landscape, the sea, a doorway, a monster.
- Use it when: Immersion, awe, solitude, an invitation into a world; stories where the viewer should feel present.
- It fails when: The figure is so large or detailed it becomes the subject, or turns enough that we read its face instead.
- Not to confuse with: Gaze cue sends the eye to a target the figure looks at; a viewer stand-in puts the viewer in the figure's place. Direct gaze is the opposite: the figure looks out at us. First-person view goes one step further: no figure, only the viewer's own hands or feet in shot.
- Also serves: Infer through Social
- Foundation: Caspar David Friedrich's Rückenfigur (Wanderer above the Sea of Fog, c. 1818); Koerner, Caspar David Friedrich and the Subject of Landscape (1990)
- Related: Gaze cue, Direct gaze, Scale disparity

#### Sequence through Cultural

**Reading path** `reading-path`

The layout follows the reader's learned direction: top left to bottom right in Latin scripts, right to left in manga.

- Why it works: Reading habits steer the eye even in images, so placing elements along that route sets the order they are met.
- What changes: position
- How to spot it: Z or F shaped layouts, a story laid out left to right, manga pages read right to left.
- Use it when: Before and after, cause and effect, multi-step stories; any page with an order of reading.
- It fails when: The order of information fights the reading direction, like the after placed before the before.
- Also serves: Sequence through Space
- Foundation: Arnold, the Gutenberg diagram (newspaper design); Nielsen, the F-shaped reading pattern (2006)
- Related: Size hierarchy, Panel rhythm

**Pointer** `pointer`

A drawn arrow, cursor, tail or line points at something, so the eye goes there next.

- Why it works: Arrows are learned signs, but we follow them almost as automatically as a gaze.
- What changes: direction
- How to spot it: Arrows, cursors, tooltip tails, leader lines that point rather than label.
- Use it when: Interfaces, explainers, step-by-step diagrams.
- It fails when: Too many arrows compete, or they point at nothing important.
- Not to confuse with: Gaze cues and body vectors point with people, leading lines with the scene; a pointer is a sign added to point.
- Foundation: Tipples, orienting to uninformative arrows (2002)
- Related: Leading lines, Gaze cue, Annotation

#### Sequence through Time

**Temporal synthesis** `temporal-synthesis`

Several moments compressed into one image: multiple exposures, motion trails, stroboscopic sequences, before and after in one frame.

- Why it works: Seeing stages side by side lets the eye read change as a sequence inside a still picture.
- What changes: time
- How to spot it: Repeated ghosted figures, step-by-step poses in one frame, a split scene showing two times.
- Use it when: Showing process, growth, motion, cause and effect.
- It fails when: The stages overlap so much they can't be told apart.
- Not to confuse with: Temporal synthesis compresses several moments of one process; a split comparison sets two states side by side to compare them.
- Foundation: Marey's chronophotography (1880s)
- Related: Implied motion, Repetition

**Staging** `staging`

One idea presented at a time, posed and timed so it cannot be missed before the next begins.

- Why it works: Attention handles one clear action at a time; staging removes competition across time, the way isolation does across space.
- What changes: timing, pose
- How to spot it: In motion: one element moves while the others hold still, clear poses, pauses before the next beat.
- Use it when: Animated explainers, interface transitions, character animation.
- It fails when: Several things move at once and the eye doesn't know where to go.
- Foundation: Thomas & Johnston, The Illusion of Life (1981)
- Related: Progressive reveal, Isolation in void

**Progressive reveal** `progressive-reveal`

Information appears in steps (build animations, scroll reveals, layers switched on), so the order of understanding is controlled.

- Why it works: Showing parts in sequence stops the viewer from reading everything at once and missing the argument.
- What changes: timing
- How to spot it: Charts that build series by series, diagrams that assemble, text that arrives line by line.
- Use it when: Explaining a complex system or argument; presentations; research animations.
- It fails when: Each step is too slow, or the final full picture is never shown.
- Foundation: Progressive disclosure in interface design (Nielsen, 2006); Segel & Heer (2010)
- Related: Staging, Annotation

**Panel rhythm** `panel-rhythm`

In comics and manga, the size, number and spacing of panels control how fast or slow time passes.

- Why it works: Readers linger on wide, empty panels and rush through rows of small ones, so the page layout becomes the clock.
- What changes: panel size, gutter width, count
- How to spot it: A full-page splash after small fast panels; long horizontal panels for silence; tight grids for action.
- Use it when: Manga and comics pacing; storyboards; any sequential layout.
- It fails when: Every panel is the same size, so the story has no tempo.
- Foundation: McCloud, Understanding Comics (1993)
- Related: Gutter closure, Reading path

#### Sequence through Encoding

**Layering and separation** `layering-separation`

Data drawn in strong ink and structure (grids, axes, frames) in faint ink, so the eye reads the data first.

- Why it works: Contrast sorts the image into layers, and the faintest layer is read last, or only when needed.
- What changes: value, weight
- How to spot it: Light gray gridlines, thin axes, bold data marks.
- Use it when: Dense charts and diagrams; maps; any image with scaffolding.
- It fails when: Gridlines and frames are as heavy as the data, so everything competes.
- Foundation: Tufte, Envisioning Information (1990)
- Related: Highlight and mute, Size hierarchy

**Annotation** `annotation`

Short notes placed on the chart at the moment that matters, telling the reader where to look and what it means.

- Why it works: A note in the right spot sets both the order of reading and the takeaway.
- What changes: text, position
- How to spot it: Callouts with leader lines, labeled events on a timeline, a sentence beside a peak.
- Use it when: Explanatory charts for non-experts; research visuals that make one claim.
- It fails when: Too many notes, or notes that repeat what the chart already shows.
- Not to confuse with: Direct labeling names a mark; annotation adds a takeaway or an interpretation.
- Foundation: Segel & Heer, Narrative Visualization (2010)
- Related: Direct labeling, Highlight and mute

### Infer

#### Infer through Feature

**Arbitrary color** `arbitrary-color`

A color that cannot be natural (green skin, a blue horse) used so the color itself carries the meaning or feeling.

- Why it works: An impossible color can't be read as description, so the viewer reads it as expression or symbol.
- What changes: hue
- How to spot it: Figures or things painted in colors they never have, consistently and on purpose.
- Use it when: Emotion, the uncanny, the spiritual, a strong signature palette.
- It fails when: It looks like a color error rather than a choice.
- Not to confuse with: A saturation accent picks one thing out; arbitrary color changes what the thing means. A whole image washed in one unnatural color counts when the tint carries the feeling (a blood-red night, a city gone green); an ordinary color grade, sepia or a duotone print does not. Tone on tone keeps a picture inside one color family by choice; label arbitrary color only when the hue itself is one the subject could not have and carries the meaning.
- Foundation: Fauvism (Matisse, 1905); Franz Marc's blue horses (1911)
- Related: Saturation accent, Learned symbol

Also used this way: Tone on tone (main entry under Group).

#### Infer through Space

**Crop at the edge** `crop-at-edge`

The subject is cut by the frame, so the viewer completes it outside the picture.

- Why it works: A cropped form keeps going in the mind, adding tension, movement and a sense of a larger world.
- What changes: framing
- How to spot it: Faces, bodies or objects cut by the frame edge, often off center.
- Use it when: Intimacy, motion, scale, a feeling that the moment continues beyond the image.
- It fails when: The crop removes what identifies the subject, or looks accidental.
- Not to confuse with: Not every close-up: only crops that add tension or scale, or make the scene feel like it continues past the frame. Frame break is the opposite: the subject crosses the frame and comes toward us instead of being cut by it.
- Foundation: Degas's cropped compositions, shaped by Japanese prints and photography
- Related: Closure, Occlusion, Frame break

**Occlusion** `occlusion`

One thing partly hides another, and the viewer completes what is hidden.

- Why it works: We automatically complete objects behind others, and filling in makes the hidden part feel present and charged.
- What changes: overlap
- How to spot it: Faces behind hands, objects behind veils or edges, things peeking out from behind something.
- Use it when: Mystery, modesty, teasing a reveal, depth.
- It fails when: So much is hidden that the subject can't be identified.
- Also serves: Sequence through Space
- Foundation: Michotte, amodal completion (1964)
- Related: Crop at the edge, Closure, Depth layering

**Forced perspective** `forced-perspective`

Objects placed at different distances so they seem to touch or interact at an impossible scale.

- Why it works: Without clear depth cues the eye reads size relations at face value, and the false relation becomes the idea.
- What changes: depth, size
- How to spot it: A person holding up a building, a giant hand in the foreground, a tiny figure on a huge object.
- Use it when: Playful surprise, product as hero, scale jokes.
- It fails when: The trick shows (misaligned contact points, mismatched focus), so it reads as fake.
- Foundation: Trick photography and film practice
- Related: Scale disparity, Depth layering

**Transparency** `transparency`

A layer reads as glass, film or water because of how it bends, tints or blurs what is behind it.

- Why it works: When the colors and edges behind a surface shift in a consistent way, the eye infers a see-through layer and its material.
- What changes: overlap, blur, tint
- How to spot it: Frosted glass panels, refraction distortions, translucent overlapping shapes, water surfaces.
- Use it when: Layered interfaces, material and depth without shadow, softness.
- It fails when: What is behind the layer is too busy or too plain to show the effect.
- Foundation: Metelli, The Perception of Transparency (1974)
- Related: Occlusion, Depth layering

**Cast-light pattern** `cast-light-pattern` (new in v1.4)

Light falling through something outside the frame (blinds, a window grid, leaves, lace) lays a shaped pattern of light and shadow across the scene and the people in it.

- Why it works: A shaped patch of light implies its source and the space beyond the frame, and the stripes or dapples wrap over every surface they cross, binding the scene together.
- What changes: shape, direction
- How to spot it: Venetian-blind stripes across a face or a wall, a window's squares on the floor, leaf dapple, a lace shadow on skin, late sun through a grille.
- Use it when: Mood and time of day, noir tension, warmth and stillness in interiors; implying a world outside the frame.
- It fails when: The pattern is too faint to read as shaped light, or so busy it breaks up the subject.
- Not to confuse with: Chiaroscuro models form with light and dark; a cast-light pattern is a shape thrown by light from an unseen source. Repetition repeats objects; here the repeated shape is the light itself. Glow is light coming from the thing; cast light comes from outside the frame.
- Also serves: Group through Feature
- Foundation: Window and blind patterns in film noir lighting (the cookie, or cucoloris); Baxandall, Shadows and Enlightenment (1995)
- Related: Chiaroscuro, Glow, Repetition

**Warped grid** `warped-grid` (new in v1.4)

A regular grid or mesh bent out of true, so its distortion shows a force, a field or a curved surface that is otherwise invisible.

- Why it works: We know the grid should be straight and even, so every bend reads as something pushing on it; the deformation draws the invisible shape.
- What changes: shape, density
- How to spot it: Spacetime wells under a planet, gravity funnels, wireframe terrain, graph paper bulging around an object, a mesh warp in type.
- Use it when: Physics and cosmology, invisible forces, data surfaces, the pull or influence of one thing on its surroundings.
- It fails when: The grid is too faint or irregular to read as once straight, or the warp is decoration and shows nothing.
- Not to confuse with: Leading lines converge on a subject; a warped grid bends around a cause. Density emergence builds a form out of many marks; a warped grid shows a form by deforming a known regular structure. Depth layering stacks flat planes; a warped grid curves one.
- Also serves: Infer through Encoding
- Foundation: D'Arcy Thompson's transformation grids, On Growth and Form (1917); embedding diagrams of curved spacetime (Misner, Thorne and Wheeler, Gravitation, 1973)
- Related: Density emergence, Leading lines, Depth layering

Also used this way: Frame break (main entry under Differentiate), First-person view (main entry under Infer).

#### Infer through Gestalt

**Closure** `closure`

An incomplete shape that the viewer completes: an outline with gaps, a form implied by fragments or by the space around it.

- Why it works: The mind fills gaps to make whole, simple forms, and that small effort makes the image stick.
- What changes: omission
- How to spot it: Logos and illustrations with missing lines that still read as complete; shapes implied by the marks around them.
- Use it when: Memorable marks, minimal logos, involving the viewer.
- It fails when: Too much is missing, so the form can't be completed.
- Not to confuse with: Density emergence is a complete form built up from many marks (string art, point clouds): nothing is missing for the viewer to fill in, so it isn't closure.
- Foundation: Gestalt closure; Kanizsa's illusory contours (1955)
- Related: Figure-ground reversal, Crop at the edge, Occlusion, Density emergence

**Figure-ground reversal** `figure-ground-reversal`

An image with two readings, depending on which part you see as the shape and which as the background.

- Why it works: The eye can hold only one figure at a time, and discovering the second reading gives a small jolt of reward.
- What changes: figure and ground
- How to spot it: Negative-space forms: an arrow between letters, two faces that are also a vase, an animal hidden in the gap.
- Use it when: Clever logos, double meanings, posters that reward a second look.
- It fails when: The hidden shape is too hard to find, or the main reading collapses.
- Foundation: Rubin, Synsoplevede Figurer (1915)
- Related: Closure, Silhouette, Hidden image

**Hidden image** `hidden-image` (new in v1.3)

A second picture concealed inside the first: a landscape that is also a face, a crowd made of tiny figures, a figure painted faintly into a cloud. It reads one way at a glance and another on a closer look.

- Why it works: Once the eye finds the second reading it can't unsee it, and the discovery makes the viewer an accomplice.
- What changes: arrangement, scale, value
- How to spot it: Heads composed of fruit or bodies, a profile in the rocks, textures that resolve into faces up close.
- Use it when: Rewarding a second look; mystery, surreal or esoteric themes; marks with a secret.
- It fails when: The hidden picture is too faint to find, or so obvious it isn't hidden.
- Not to confuse with: Figure-ground reversal flips which shape is figure and which is background; face schema is an accidental face in an object; a hidden image is a deliberate second picture built into the first.
- Foundation: Arcimboldo's composite heads (1560s–1590s); Dalí's double images (1930s); multistable perception in Gestalt psychology
- Related: Figure-ground reversal, Face schema, Closure

Also used this way: Self-similarity (main entry under Group).

#### Infer through Social

**Anthropomorphism** `anthropomorphism`

Objects, animals or brands given human traits: faces, gestures, posture, emotion.

- Why it works: We readily see minds and feelings in things that act human, and relate to them as characters.
- What changes: pose, features
- How to spot it: Objects with eyes or limbs, animals in human clothes or poses, products acting like people.
- Use it when: Making brands friendly, mascots, humor, empathy for non-human subjects.
- It fails when: The human traits turn uncanny, or the object's own identity gets lost.
- Not to confuse with: Decide by how it reads. A character that reads first as having a face or human manner is anthropomorphism; a face that emerges from parts that read first as something else is face schema.
- Foundation: Epley, Waytz & Cacioppo, On Seeing Human (2007)
- Related: Face schema, Exaggeration

**First-person view** `first-person-view` (new in v1.4)

The picture is taken from the viewer's own eyes: their hands, feet, lap or the thing they hold come into the frame from the bottom edge, so the viewer becomes the one in the scene.

- Why it works: Limbs cut by the lower edge at the angle we see our own are read as ours; the scene stops being looked at and starts being lived in.
- What changes: pose, framing
- How to spot it: Hands holding a cup, a phone, a book or a weapon from below; feet up on a bed or a sofa; a laptop on a lap; game and POV camera shots.
- Use it when: Immersion, intimacy, everyday ritual, a product in use; making the viewer the protagonist.
- It fails when: The limbs are posed or lit like someone else's, or so small that the frame reads as an ordinary still life.
- Not to confuse with: Viewer stand-in shows a figure from behind that we step into; first-person view puts us in our own body, with only our limbs in shot. A hand that is itself the subject (a hand study, a product held up for the camera by a model) is not first-person view. Viewpoint is the camera's height or angle; first-person view is the camera at the viewer's eyes with the viewer's own body in frame.
- Also serves: Infer through Space
- Foundation: The point-of-view shot in film (Branigan, Point of View in the Cinema, 1984); Lady in the Lake (1947), a whole film shot first-person
- Related: Viewer stand-in, Viewpoint, Direct gaze

**Implied presence** `implied-presence` (new in v1.6)

No one is in the picture, but traces show that someone was just here or is about to come back (an unmade bed, a steaming cup, shoes kicked off, a coat on an empty chair, an outfit laid out in the shape of a body), so the viewer pictures the missing person.

- Why it works: We read objects as evidence of use; a dent in a pillow or a half-finished drink sets the mind to fill in the absent body and the moment just before.
- What changes: omission, time
- How to spot it: Unmade beds, a laptop and headphones left on the duvet, a half-drunk glass, slippers by the bed, a coat on an empty chair, a book left open, a shadow burned into a wall, an outfit laid flat in body order.
- Use it when: Intimacy, nostalgia, loneliness or loss; lived-in interiors; stories told after the fact; inviting the viewer to imagine the person.
- It fails when: The objects are styled so neatly they read as a product shot, or nothing points to a person or a moment. A person in the frame, even a small one, makes it something else, unless the trace plainly belongs to someone else who is gone.
- Not to confuse with: Viewer stand-in shows a figure from behind and first-person view shows the viewer's own body; implied presence shows no one at all, only traces. Pregnant moment freezes an action just before it resolves; implied presence shows the scene after the person has gone. Closure completes a missing part of a shape; implied presence completes a missing person from evidence. A sleeping pet alone doesn't count unless the scene around it points to the owner.
- Also serves: Infer through Time
- Foundation: The empty-chair tradition in painting (Van Gogh, Gauguin's Chair, 1888); Barthes, Camera Lucida (1980), on the photograph as a trace of what has been
- Related: Viewer stand-in, First-person view, Pregnant moment, Closure

Also used this way: Face schema (main entry under Group), Viewpoint (main entry under Differentiate), Viewer stand-in (main entry under Sequence).

#### Infer through Semantic

**Side-by-side analogy** `side-by-side-analogy`

Two things placed next to each other so the viewer transfers the qualities of one to the other.

- Why it works: Pairing invites comparison, and when the likeness is clear, the meaning of one rubs off on the other.
- What changes: subject meaning
- How to spot it: A product beside an animal, object or scene that shares its shape or quality.
- Use it when: Explaining a benefit without words; making the abstract concrete.
- It fails when: The likeness isn't visible, so the pairing seems random.
- Not to confuse with: Fusion merges the two into one form; an analogy keeps them separate.
- Foundation: Phillips & McQuarrie, juxtaposition for similarity (2004)
- Related: Semantic contrast, Fusion, Form substitution

**Fusion** `fusion`

Two objects merged into one hybrid form, so both meanings arrive at once.

- Why it works: The impossible object stops the eye, and decoding its two halves delivers the idea.
- What changes: form, subject meaning
- How to spot it: A lightbulb that is also a pear, a hand made of trees, a building that is also a face.
- Use it when: One striking concept; editorial illustration; posters.
- It fails when: One half is unrecognizable, or the combination means nothing beyond its strangeness.
- Not to confuse with: If both identities stay visible in one form, it's fusion; if one object is absent and only its shape survives in another object's place, it's form substitution. Label invented hybrids where the merge is the idea; established creatures (a griffin, a mermaid, a harpy) are subjects, not fusion, unless the image makes the merge itself the point.
- Also serves: Group through Semantic
- Foundation: Phillips & McQuarrie, fusion (2004)
- Related: Side-by-side analogy, Form substitution, Semantic contrast

**Form substitution** `form-substitution`

An expected object is replaced by another of similar shape (a cigarette drawn as a bullet, a road as a ribbon), and the swap carries the meaning.

- Why it works: The familiar shape lets the eye accept the replacement; then the mind notices the swap and connects the two meanings.
- What changes: subject, form
- How to spot it: Something sitting where a different, similarly shaped thing belongs.
- Use it when: Warnings, wit, condensing a message into one image.
- It fails when: The shapes are not similar enough for the swap to read at first glance.
- Not to confuse with: If both identities stay visible in one form, it's fusion; if one object is absent and only its shape survives in another object's place, it's form substitution.
- Foundation: Phillips & McQuarrie, replacement (2004)
- Related: Fusion, Contextual metaphor

**Contextual metaphor** `contextual-metaphor`

An ordinary object or small scene placed in a setting or story that gives it a new meaning: sneakers on a winner's podium, a tie hung like a noose, mice raiding the cheese.

- Why it works: The setting switches on a familiar script, and the object is read through it.
- What changes: context
- How to spot it: The objects are unchanged; the situation or little story around them does the work.
- Use it when: Saying something about a product or idea without altering it; dry wit.
- It fails when: The setting is too unfamiliar for the script to fire.
- Not to confuse with: In form substitution the object changes; in a contextual metaphor only its surroundings do.
- Foundation: Phillips & McQuarrie, replacement (2004); Barthes, Rhetoric of the Image (1964)
- Related: Form substitution, Genre quotation

**Anchorage** `anchorage`

Words fix how the picture is read: a headline or caption picks which of the image's possible meanings counts.

- Why it works: Images are open to many readings; a line of text closes them down to one, and the pairing becomes the idea.
- What changes: text
- How to spot it: A picture whose meaning turns on its caption, like 'Sensitive dependencies' over a butterfly; headlines that reframe an ordinary photo.
- Use it when: Campaigns, editorial, research visuals where the image must mean one thing.
- It fails when: The text just repeats the picture, or contradicts it to no purpose.
- Not to confuse with: Annotation explains data at a point; anchorage decides what a whole picture means.
- Foundation: Barthes, anchorage in Rhetoric of the Image (1964)
- Related: Contextual metaphor, Annotation

**Rebus** `rebus` (new in v1.3)

Small pictures stand in for words or sounds inside a line of text or a name, so the viewer reads the pictures as language.

- Why it works: Swapping a word for a picture slows reading for a moment, and the small puzzle makes the message stick.
- What changes: sign, position
- How to spot it: An eye for 'I', a heart for 'love', icons or emoji set into a sentence, a name spelled partly in pictures.
- Use it when: Playful campaigns, memorable logos and taglines, lively interface copy.
- It fails when: The picture could be read as several words, or the line takes too long to decode.
- Not to confuse with: Pictorial units count things with pictures; a rebus reads pictures as words. Anchorage uses words to fix a picture's meaning; a rebus uses pictures in place of words.
- Foundation: The rebus tradition; Paul Rand's Eye-Bee-M poster for IBM (1981); Milton Glaser's I ♥ NY (1977)
- Related: Learned symbol, Word as image, Anchorage

**Word as image** `word-as-image` (new in v1.3)

Lettering shaped, placed or broken so its form acts out what the word says: text massed into the shape it names, a word that cracks where it says 'break'.

- Why it works: Reading and seeing happen at once, so the meaning lands twice in the time of one.
- What changes: letterform, arrangement
- How to spot it: Calligrams, a word melting or glitching to match its meaning, a name drawn into the object it names.
- Use it when: Logotypes, titles and posters where one word has to carry the idea.
- It fails when: The word stops being readable, or the effect is decoration that doesn't match the meaning.
- Not to confuse with: Sound lettering draws sound effects into a scene; word as image makes any word act out its meaning. A rebus swaps words for pictures; word as image turns the word itself into the picture.
- Foundation: Apollinaire, Calligrammes (1918); Ji Lee, Word as Image (2011)
- Related: Sound lettering, Rebus, Fusion

**Graphic overlay** `graphic-overlay` (new in v1.4)

A flat drawn or graphic layer laid over a finished picture (usually a photograph, also a painting, illustration or render) and kept visibly separate from it (line art, glyphs, grids, callouts, type), often lined up with a real feature so the two layers read together.

- Why it works: The eye takes the picture underneath as the world and the flat marks as a comment on it; where they line up, the drawing seems to reveal something hidden in it.
- What changes: mark, alignment
- How to spot it: Line art traced over a portrait, a sigil drawn on a landscape, circles and arrows annotating a photo, interface callouts floating over a face, doodles or a star grid laid over a painting, light ribbons drawn over an archive photo.
- Use it when: Annotation, revelation, hidden structure, mixing documentary and graphic registers, tech or occult overlays.
- It fails when: The overlay melts into the picture (then it is retouching), ignores it so the two layers don't relate, or is drawn in the same hand and medium as the picture (then it is part of the drawing).
- Not to confuse with: Collage cuts and joins pieces of different pictures; graphic overlay keeps one photo whole and draws on top of it. Transparency is a see-through material inside the scene; graphic overlay is a flat layer on the picture surface. Direct labeling names the marks of a chart; graphic overlay draws on a photograph. Text laid over a photo only as a caption or title is not graphic overlay unless it relates to what it covers.
- Also serves: Differentiate through Space
- Foundation: Layering and separation in information design (Tufte, Envisioning Information, 1990); annotated and drawn-over photographs in photomontage
- Related: Transparency, Direct labeling, Semantic contrast

Also used this way: Exaggeration (main entry under Differentiate).

#### Infer through Cultural

**Genre quotation** `genre-quotation`

The image borrows the codes of a recognizable genre (film noir, pulp cover, propaganda poster, scientific plate, fashion cover) so its content is read through that genre.

- Why it works: Genre codes carry ready-made associations, so the borrowed look brings its meaning along.
- What changes: style codes
- How to spot it: Typography, layout, color and photography that clearly belong to another kind of artifact.
- Use it when: Instant tone; irony; positioning something against a known reference.
- It fails when: The audience doesn't know the genre, or the borrowing is pure costume with nothing to say.
- Not to confuse with: Count it only when the borrowed genre is foreign to the artifact (a T-shirt dressed as an x-ray, a brand page dressed as a museum label). An artifact in its own category's look, like a science brand that looks scientific, is not quoting. Visual quotation cites one specific famous image.
- Foundation: Genette, Palimpsests (1982), on pastiche
- Related: Visual quotation, Learned symbol, Contextual metaphor

**Visual quotation** `visual-quotation`

A specific famous image (a pose, painting, film frame or photograph) recreated or referenced, so its meaning transfers.

- Why it works: Recognizing the original adds a second layer: the new image is read against the one it cites.
- What changes: composition, pose
- How to spot it: A recognizable composition or pose restaged with new subjects.
- Use it when: Homage, parody, borrowing authority or nostalgia.
- It fails when: The original is not recognized, or the quote is used without adding anything.
- Foundation: Kristeva, intertextuality (late 1960s)
- Related: Genre quotation

**Visible facture** `visible-facture`

The traces of a process are left visible and carry meaning: brush strokes, construction lines, print misregistration, halftone and dither, compression glitches, torn edges.

- Why it works: Traces of making say how the image came to be (by hand, by a press, by a machine, by something breaking), and that becomes part of its meaning.
- What changes: texture, mark
- How to spot it: Expressive brushwork, visible sketch lines, halftone dots, riso misprints, datamosh and pixel corruption, paper texture.
- Use it when: Warmth, craft, authenticity, process, or machine-ness and decay; pushing back against slick digital polish.
- It fails when: The trace is decoration with nothing to say, or it hides the subject.
- Not to confuse with: Label it when the trace says something (handmade, printed, machine-made, broken), not just because the medium shows; every watercolor has washes. If the texture mainly separates figure from ground, it's texture against flat; if it borrows a medium's look to evoke another era or genre, it's genre quotation. Dots that build a form no outline draws are density emergence; dots that show how the image was printed or made are visible facture.
- Also serves: Differentiate through Feature
- Foundation: Faktura in the Russian avant-garde (1910s)
- Related: Texture against flat, Genre quotation

**Learned symbol** `learned-symbol`

A sign whose meaning comes from convention, not appearance: a heart, a cross, red for stop, a dove, a laurel.

- Why it works: Once learned, symbols are read instantly and carry large meanings in small marks.
- What changes: sign
- How to spot it: Icons and objects that stand for an idea rather than for themselves.
- Use it when: Fast communication, signage, iconography, layered meaning.
- It fails when: The audience doesn't share the convention, or the symbol is a cliché that adds nothing.
- Not to confuse with: Label it only when a specific symbol's conventional meaning is how the image communicates: a heart for love, a crucifixion pose, a skull for death. Not for decorative iconography or diagram notation (arrows, valves, flowchart shapes). Small story scenes whose situation carries the meaning are contextual metaphors. In religious and esoteric art: label a symbol when it carries a meaning beyond the subject (the dove, Death's scythe, a cross between antlers); not when it only tells you who the subject is (Diana's crescent, a saint's halo, Orpheus's lyre).
- Foundation: Peirce's icon, index and symbol (late 19th century); Barthes, Mythologies (1957)
- Related: Genre quotation, Contextual metaphor, Halo framing, Rebus

**Sound lettering** `sound-lettering`

Sound effects drawn as lettering that becomes part of the image: the shape, weight and size of the letters carry the noise.

- Why it works: Readers hear the drawn word, and its form adds force, speed and texture to a silent picture.
- What changes: letterform, size
- How to spot it: Manga and comics sound effects, words that crack, wobble or explode with the action.
- Use it when: Comics and manga, playful editorial, packaging with attitude.
- It fails when: The lettering competes with the drawing instead of joining it.
- Foundation: Comics practice; McCloud, Understanding Comics (1993)
- Related: Implied motion, Panel rhythm, Word as image

#### Infer through Time

**Gutter closure** `gutter-closure`

Two frames or panels shown in sequence, and the viewer imagines what happened between them.

- Why it works: Readers automatically connect consecutive images into one event, supplying the missing action.
- What changes: omission, time
- How to spot it: A raised fist in one panel and a fallen figure in the next; a cut that skips the action.
- Use it when: Comics and manga, storyboards, editing, before and after pairs.
- It fails when: The gap is too big to bridge, so the sequence reads as unrelated images.
- Foundation: McCloud, closure, Understanding Comics (1993)
- Related: Panel rhythm, Closure, Match cut

**Implied motion** `implied-motion`

A still image made to read as moving: speed lines, motion blur, frozen mid-action poses, things caught falling.

- Why it works: The visual system fills in the movement it expects from these cues.
- What changes: blur, lines, pose
- How to spot it: Manga speed lines, streaks, poses at the peak of an action, splashes frozen in the air.
- Use it when: Energy and action in stills; product dynamism; sport.
- It fails when: The cues contradict each other, or the blur hides the subject.
- Not to confuse with: Implied motion shows movement under way; a pregnant moment stops just before the outcome. Body vector is where a pose sends the eye.
- Foundation: Futurist painting (Balla, Dynamism of a Dog on a Leash, 1912); manga speed lines
- Related: Temporal synthesis, Body vector, Common fate

**Pregnant moment** `pregnant-moment`

A single image caught just before the decisive action, so the viewer supplies what happens next.

- Why it works: The instant of greatest tension holds the before and the after at once, and the mind completes the action.
- What changes: time
- How to spot it: A foot about to land, a glass about to fall, a hand reaching for the door.
- Use it when: Suspense, story in one frame, narrative illustration.
- It fails when: The outcome isn't predictable enough to imagine, or the moment is already past.
- Not to confuse with: Implied motion shows movement under way; the pregnant moment stops just before the outcome.
- Foundation: Lessing, Laocoön (1766)
- Related: Implied motion, Gutter closure

**Match cut** `match-cut`

A cut or transition between two different things that share a shape or motion, so they read as one continuous thing.

- Why it works: Continuity of shape across the cut makes the mind connect the two subjects and carry meaning from one to the other.
- What changes: shape, motion
- How to spot it: In video: a round object becoming another round object, a gesture finished by a different subject. In stills: diptychs with matched shapes.
- Use it when: Transitions in motion work, connecting ideas, time jumps.
- It fails when: The shapes or motions don't line up, so the cut feels arbitrary.
- Also serves: Group through Time
- Foundation: Film editing; the bone-to-satellite cut in Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968)
- Related: Gutter closure, Form substitution, Side-by-side analogy

Also used this way: Implied presence (main entry under Infer).

#### Infer through Encoding

**Pictorial units** `pictorial-units`

Data counted with small pictures of the thing being counted: one figure stands for a thousand people.

- Why it works: The shape says what is counted and the repetition says how many, so the chart reads without a legend.
- What changes: count, pictogram
- How to spot it: Rows of identical icons standing for quantities.
- Use it when: Public-facing statistics; quantities people should feel rather than estimate.
- It fails when: Icons are scaled up in size instead of repeated, so their area misleads.
- Foundation: Otto and Marie Neurath, Isotype (from the 1920s)
- Related: Repetition, Small multiples

Also used this way: Warped grid (main entry under Infer).

## How it looks: the four look fields

Each reference also gets one answer per field, chosen from these lists. They describe the look; techniques describe the mechanism.

- **Medium**: Photo, Painting, Drawing (pencil, ink, charcoal by hand), Print (engraving, etching, woodcut, screenprint), Digital illustration, Comic or manga, Animation (anime, cel or cartoon frames and clips; motion design keeps its own medium), 3D render, Interface (app, website, software screen), Graphic design (poster, brand, layout, packaging), Typography (type or lettering is the image), Diagram or chart, Generative (made by code, simulation or plotter), Collage, Mixed
- **Camera angle**: Eye level, Low angle (looking up), High angle (looking down), Overhead (straight down), Worm's-eye (from the ground, extreme low), Tilted (horizon on a slant), Flat (no camera: layouts, interfaces, flat graphics)
- **Framing**: Extreme close-up (a detail), Close-up (a face or object filling the frame), Medium (waist up, or an object with some setting), Full (the whole figure or object), Wide (subject small in its setting), All-over (pattern or texture filling the frame), Layout (a page, screen, board or poster as a whole)
- **Lighting**: High-key (bright, few shadows), Low-key (mostly dark), Soft (diffused), Hard (crisp shadows), Backlit, Golden hour, Night, Lamplight (a room lit by its own lamps or candles, warm pools of light), Neon (colored artificial light), Self-lit (the glowing things in the picture are the light: screens, lanterns, magic, light art), Flat (no modeled light: graphics, interfaces)

## Labeling rules

1. Label the mechanism the image depends on. Test: if you removed it, would the image stop working? If not, don't label it.
2. Give 0 to 3 techniques per reference; most get 1 or 2. 'None' is a valid and useful answer for plain screenshots, documentation photos, and references saved for one small detail.
3. Every photo has some light and dark and every layout has some size differences. Label chiaroscuro or size hierarchy only when that contrast is the idea.
4. When two techniques fit the same element, use their 'Not to confuse with' notes to pick one. One element may carry two labels only when it does two different jobs (a sheer veil both hides and tints what's behind it: occlusion and transparency; a neon sign is both the light in the scene and the words that fix its meaning: glow and anchorage), never for two readings of the same job. When a newer technique names exactly the job an older label describes on the same element (a figure seen from behind: viewer stand-in, not gaze cue), keep only the more exact one.
5. For collages, grids and moodboards, label the technique of the arrangement (repetition, similarity, color blocking). If one idea carries the board (the flower-iris eye in a brand identity, a mark repeated on every tile), label that too, as a second label. A designed system (a brand board, a product family built to match) counts as similarity; a board of unrelated pieces collected for mood is 'none' unless its arrangement or one standout piece has an idea of its own.
6. Name the carrier: the specific element doing the work, in a few words ("the single lit face against black").
7. Pick the path that matches how this image uses the technique. Most images use the first path listed.
8. Confidence: 0.9 obvious and central; 0.7 clearly present and doing work; 0.5 a real supporting mechanism you would defend, not a faint presence. If you hesitate to defend it, leave it out.
9. Never label aesthetics, styles or subjects (Y2K, brutalism, cyberpunk, minimal). Those belong in the description and tags.
10. For video you see one poster frame. Label what the frame shows. Use Time techniques only when the frame itself shows them (trails, a staged beat) or the description states the motion plainly; motion it only guesses at ('likely', 'probably') doesn't count.
11. Describe how the image looks in the four look fields (medium, camera angle, framing, lighting), choosing each answer from its fixed list. They describe the look; techniques describe the mechanism. A low camera is always noted as an angle, and labeled viewpoint only when the angle is what the image depends on.

## Working a brief

1. Read the brief for what it needs the viewer to do, and name the operation (sometimes two).
2. Choose the domain to work through. The material often decides: a photo shoot works through Feature and Space, a campaign line through Semantic, a chart through Encoding.
3. Pick two or three candidate techniques. Say why each fits and what it would look like in this job.
4. Pull references from your own library that execute them and look at them before designing.
5. Design. Cite the technique and the reference numbers behind each decision.
6. Record the path (brief, techniques chosen and why, techniques rejected and why) so the reasoning can be reviewed later.
